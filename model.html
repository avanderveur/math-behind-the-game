Formula Translation

Reading the Calculus in Plain English

Each formula below is shown with how to read it and what it means in the baseball model.

Position Motion Model

r(t) = <x(t), y(t)>

Read as: “r of t equals the ordered pair x of t and y of t.”

This means the ball’s position has two parts: horizontal position and vertical height.

Horizontal Motion Distance

x(t) = v₀cos(θ)t

Read as: “x of t equals v naught cosine theta times t.”

This estimates how far the ball travels horizontally based on starting speed, launch angle, and time.

Vertical Motion Height

y(t) = y₀ + v₀sin(θ)t − 16t²

Read as: “y of t equals y naught plus v naught sine theta times t minus sixteen t squared.”

This estimates the ball’s height over time. The final term models gravity pulling the ball downward.

Velocity Derivative

r′(t) = velocity

Read as: “r prime of t equals velocity.”

Velocity is the derivative of position. It describes how fast the ball’s position is changing at a specific moment.

Acceleration Gravity

r″(t) = acceleration

Read as: “r double prime of t equals acceleration.”

Acceleration is the derivative of velocity. In this simplified model, the main acceleration is gravity pulling downward.

Maximum Height Critical Point

y′(t) = 0

Read as: “y prime of t equals zero.”

This happens at the apex of the ball’s flight. The ball is no longer rising, but it has not started falling yet.

Pitch Timing Reaction Window

t ≈ d ÷ v

Read as: “t is approximately distance divided by velocity.”

This estimates how long the hitter has to react to a pitch. As pitch velocity increases, reaction time decreases.

Spray Chart Direction

φ = spray direction

Read as: “phi equals spray direction.”

Spray direction describes whether the ball travels toward left field, center field, or right field.

Model Scope

What the Model Simplifies

This project uses a simplified educational model. It focuses on the calculus relationships between position, velocity, acceleration, launch angle, exit velocity, reaction time, and field direction.

A professional baseball model would also include air resistance, spin rate, wind, stadium dimensions, field conditions, player-specific data, and real tracking measurements. Those factors are outside the scope of this Math 1A project, but the simplified model is still useful because it clearly shows how calculus describes motion.

Final Connection

Calculus Turns Motion Into Meaning

In this project, derivatives explain velocity. Velocity explains motion. Motion explains baseball outcomes. That is how calculus connects to pitching, hitting, spray charts, and analytics.
